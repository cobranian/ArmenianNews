Recognized by international critics as a major figure in 20th-century art, Arshile Gorky left a profound mark on the history of painting. Alongside artists such as Willem de Kooning, Jackson Pollock, and Mark Rothko, he established himself as one of the essential initiators of Abstract Expressionism and lyrical abstraction. Yet, his innovative visual language was rooted in the landscapes of his childhood and the trials of Armenian exile.

## From Khorkom to America: Shattered Childhood and Exile

Arshile Gorky was born Vosdanik Manoug Adoian on April 15, 1904, in the village of Khorkom, located on the shores of Lake Van in the Ottoman Empire. Very early on, the household was marked by departures: in 1908, his father left the region for the United States to escape military conscription. Vosdanik received his early education in the schools of Khorkom and Van.

In 1915, in the face of the Armenian Genocide, he was forced to flee with his mother, Shushanik Ter-Martirosyan, and his sisters. The group found refuge in Russian-controlled Transcaucasia, passing through Etchmiadzin before settling in Yerevan. There, the young boy attended the diocesan school, where he studied drawing and wood engraving. These were tragic times: during the harsh winter of 1918–1919, his mother died of starvation and deprivation in Yerevan. Left to fend for himself with one of his sisters, he traveled through Tiflis and Batum before embarking for the New World in 1920. Arriving at the age of sixteen, he was reunited with his father in Boston, though the two men never managed to develop any real closeness.

## The New York Forge and the Quest for a Style

First settling in Providence, Vosdanik enrolled at the Rhode Island School of Design, then at the New England School of Art in Boston. In 1924–1925, he moved to New York. It was during this period that he reinvented his public identity by adopting the pseudonym Arshile Gorky. He attended the Grand Central School of Art, where he taught drawing and painting from 1926 to 1931. Revington Arthur was among his students, and he forged strong friendships with other painters, notably Willem de Kooning and Stuart Davis, as well as Ethel Schwabacher, who would later become his first biographer.

Throughout the 1920s and early 1930s, his art assimilated the contributions of the European avant-garde. Gorky methodically studied Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, and then the work of Paul Cézanne and Pablo Picasso, before turning to Cubism and Surrealist automatism. In the mid-1930s, during the Great Depression, he benefited from New Deal government programs, notably the Federal Art Project, which commissioned him to create a series of murals for Newark Airport.

## Armenian Memory at the Heart of Modernity

His firsthand experience of the genocide and reminiscences of his homeland formed a decisive element of his sensibility. From 1926 to 1936, he worked patiently on two versions of *The Artist and His Mother*, a double portrait inspired by a childhood photograph taken in Van, marked by the simplicity of its lines and the melancholy of a suspended dialogue.

Gradually, beginning in the 1940s, Gorky developed his singular style. His brushwork loosened to invent biomorphic forms, where sharp black lines intertwined with luminous washes of fluid color. This maturity found expression in famous works such as *Garden in Sochi* (initially conceived under the title *Garden in Khorkom*), *Waterfall* (1943), and *How My Mother's Embroidered Apron Unfolds in My Life* (1944). In 1944, Surrealist writer André Breton discovered his paintings in New York; captivated by *The Liver is the Cock's Comb* (1944), he called this monumental composition a landmark masterpiece of painting created in America and hailed the Armenian artist as a true Surrealist.

## Final Trials and the Legacy of a Pioneer

Married in 1941 to Agnes Magruder, whom he affectionately nicknamed "Mougouch" and with whom he had two daughters, Maro and Natasha, Gorky saw the end of his life darkened by a succession of tragedies. In 1946, a devastating fire destroyed his studio, consuming his library and dozens of paintings. That same year, he underwent major surgery for cancer. In 1948, a car accident broke his neck and temporarily paralyzed his right arm, the very tool of his craft. Separated from his wife, and emotionally and physically exhausted, the artist took his own life by hanging on July 21, 1948, in Sherman, Connecticut, at the age of forty-four.

His contribution remains immense. By paving the way for two generations of New York School artists, Gorky enriched universal modern art while preserving, at the core of his abstraction, the indelible echo of his native land. Today, his works are preserved in the world's greatest museums, from the National Gallery of Art in Washington and the Centre Pompidou in Paris to Tate in London and the National Gallery of Armenia.
