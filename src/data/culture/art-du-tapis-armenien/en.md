## A Millennia-Old Tradition Rooted in the Armenian Highlands

In the Armenian Highlands, the art of weaving and knotting carpets dates back to deep antiquity. Archaeological excavations have unearthed weaving tools dating to the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC at sites such as Teghut, Shresh Blur, and Shengavit. Textile fragments discovered in the Areni-1 cave, as well as remnants dating back to the 13th and 7th centuries BC found in Artik, Karmir Blur, and Ani, bear witness to this antiquity. As early as the 5th century BC, the Greek historian Herodotus recorded that the inhabitants of the Caucasus dyed their wools using plant dyes whose brilliance was dulled neither by water nor by time.

The oldest pile-knotted carpet to survive intact to this day is the Pazyryk carpet, discovered in 1949 by archaeologist Sergei Rudenko in a frozen burial mound in Siberia and preserved at the Hermitage Museum in Saint Petersburg. Dated to between the 5th and 3rd centuries BC, this piece, crafted with the Armenian double knot, features threads dyed with Armenian cochineal, *vordan karmir*. Scholars of ancient textile art, such as Ulrich Schurmann and Volkmar Gantzhorn, recognize it as a work of Armenian origin, pointing out the similarity between the horse motifs of Pazyryk and the bas-reliefs of the Armenian delegation sculpted at Persepolis.

This craft initially served practical daily needs: in traditional stone and rock dwellings lacking wooden floors, carpets warmed the floors and adorned walls, divans, and beds. Produced primarily by women within households, it formed an indispensable part of a young woman's dowry, while also adorning churches, their altars, and their vestries.

## Vocabulary, Techniques, and Iconic Motifs

The Armenian language traditionally uses two terms to refer to these textile works: the word *gorg*, which applies specifically to pile or knotted carpets, and the word *karpet*, reserved for flatweaves without pile. The term *kapert*, derived from the root *kap* meaning "knot", appears as early as the 5th century in the Armenian translation of the Gospels, notably in Matthew 9:16 and Mark 2:21. Meanwhile, the word *gorg* appears carved in an inscription from 1242–1243 on the walls of the Kaptavan church in Artsakh. In addition, medieval Arab authors such as Yaqut al-Hamawi and Abd al-Rashid al-Bakuvi note that the term *kali* or *khali*, used throughout the Muslim world to designate knotted carpets, derives from the name of the Armenian city of Karin (called Qaliqala by the Arabs).

Armenian craftsmanship is distinguished by a wide diversity of ornamental repertoires. The so-called *vishapagorg* (dragon carpets) are among the oldest and most remarkable: they combine the stylized dragon with the tree of life, the phoenix, and crenellated geometric forms. To this family are added the *artsvagorg* (eagle carpets) and the *otsagorg* (serpent carpets). Over the centuries, artisans developed a rich array of geometric, floral, and cruciform patterns, often structured around central polylobed or star-shaped medallions.

## At the Heart of Medieval Trade and Global Influence

During the medieval period, Armenia exported its productions extensively. As early as the 8th century, under Abbasid rule, annual tributes sent to Baghdad included Armenian carpets. In the 10th century, Arab chroniclers Ibn Hawqal and al-Istakhri described the renown of the textile manufactories of Dvin and Artashat, famed for the quality of their wool and the brilliance of their dyes obtained from cochineal. In the 13th century, the historian Kirakos Gandzaketsi praised the textile craftsmanship of Princess Arzu-Khatun and her daughters in Artsakh, while Marco Polo described the pieces made by Armenian artisans as the finest in the world.

The carpet trade expanded to Western Europe thanks to Cilician merchants who exported their textiles from the ports of Ayas and Sis to Florence, a fact recorded by the Italian banker Francesco Balducci Pegolotti. Armenian merchant communities established in Egypt, Poland, Ukraine, Romania, and Russia subsequently disseminated these goods. In 1604, during the deportations ordered by the Shah of Iran, Abbas I, artisans from Julfa settled in New Julfa (Isfahan), boosting trade toward India and Russia. By the 18th century, Armenian merchants even had a dedicated section at the Royal Exchange in London, along Threadneedle Street.

## Historical Trials, Memory, and Contemporary Revival

The massacres and the Armenian Genocide perpetrated between 1894 and 1923 disrupted this craft geography by destroying the weaving centers of Western Armenia and the Ottoman Empire. In the orphanages established in the Near East to take in survivors, weaving instruction made it possible to pass down the craft to the younger generations. In Ghazir, Lebanon, under the direction of Swiss missionary Jacob Künzler and the Near East Relief, four hundred orphan girls crafted a rug featuring four million knots between 1924 and 1925, depicting the Garden of Eden, which was later presented to the White House.

At the same time, the diaspora carried this heritage forward in the United States, where masters such as S. Tayryakyan and K. Taushadjyan developed the so-called "American Sarouk" style, while the A. & M. Karagheusian company opened its industrial mills in 1904. During the Soviet era, the establishment of cooperatives kept the tradition alive, with factories such as Arm Carpet in Yerevan in 1924, the Ijevan workshops in 1959, and those of Karmirgyugh in 1958.

Today, carpet weaving remains very much alive thanks to hand-weaving companies such as Tufenkian, established in Yerevan since 1994, Megerian Carpets since 2000, and the Artsakh Carpet factory founded in 2013 in Stepanakert, the same year a carpet museum also opened its doors in Shushi.
