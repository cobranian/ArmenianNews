Among the instruments that embody Armenia's musical expression, the duduk—traditionally known as *dziranapogh* (ծիրանափող), literally the "apricot horn" or "apricot pipe"—holds a central place. A true symbol of national identity, this double-reed, cylindrical-bore oboe is clearly distinguished from conical-bore instruments such as the zurna by the incomparable warmth and sweetness of its timbre. As early as the 5th century AD, the historian Moses of Chorene mentioned the instrument under its name of *dziranapogh*. Several Armenian historians and musicologists even trace its roots back to 1200 BC, while other specialists point to an attested practice during the reign of King Tigranes the Great, between 95 and 55 BC.

As ethnomusicologist Jonathan McCollum highlights, the duduk appears in numerous medieval Armenian manuscripts and is the only truly Armenian instrument to have survived continuously through the ages. In his view, its primary quality lies in its singular ability to express the dialectics and nuances of the Armenian language. This emotional power led composer Aram Khachaturian to declare: "The duduk is the only instrument whose sound makes me cry." In recognition of this living tradition, UNESCO proclaimed the duduk and its music a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005, before inscribing it on the Representative List in 2008.

## Traditional Craftsmanship Sculpted from Apricot Wood

The crafting of the duduk relies on the use of aged apricot wood, occasionally pear wood, carved and drilled with a cylindrical bore. The length of its body generally ranges between 28 and 40 centimeters—with common sizes around 30.5 to 33 centimeters depending on the desired register and key. The instrument features seven or eight finger holes on its front, while the back includes a thumb hole and sometimes an additional tuning hole, which can be covered by the player to produce a lower note.

The defining element of its acoustics lies in its broad double reed, called *ghamish* or *eghegn* (եղեգն). Cut from a flattened piece of cane measuring between 9 and 14 centimeters, it has an opening regulated by a movable ring or tuning collar. This mechanism allows the musician to fine-tune the pitch and stability of the sound. During the 1920s and 1930s, Armenian designer and composer V. G. Buni modernized the instrument by creating three orchestral variants with extended registers, including the baritone version named the "buniphone." Today, some models incorporate modern keys to facilitate chromatic playing.

## Breath Technique and Circular Breathing

The duduk player, known as a *dudukahar* (դուդուկահար), holds the reed firmly between their lips. To sustain an uninterrupted melodic line, performers employ the technique of circular breathing: the musician stores air in their cheeks to keep feeding the reed at the exact moment they inhale through the nose, with the tongue regulating the airflow.

Traditionally, the duduk is not played alone. The melody is most often supported by a second player called the *damkash*, who maintains a continuous drone (*dum*). This harmonic foundation provides constant energy and creates a deep sonic atmosphere over which melodic ornaments, lyrical songs, or pieces from the mugham genre unfold. In festive and dance repertoires, the duduk duo is paired with the rhythm of the *dhol* drum or the *daf*. Over the centuries, this music has accompanied every major milestone of Armenian society, from folk celebrations and weddings to funeral ceremonies.

## From the Great Masters to the Contemporary Revival

The modern history of the duduk is marked by illustrious masters who preserved and refined its performance practice. Among the leading 20th-century figures are Margar Margaryan, Levon Madoyan, Vatche Hovsepian, Karapet Yeghoyan, Khachik Khachatryan, and Hovhannes Kasyan. More recently, musicians such as Gevorg Dabaghyan, Sergei Karapetyan, Mkrtich Malkhasyan, Vladimir Harutyunyan, and Norayr Kartashyan have enriched this repertoire. Long associated with an exclusively male tradition, the instrument opened up in the late 20th century to female musicians including Armine Grigoryan, Anna Sargsyan, and Lilit Hovsepyan.

The figure of Djivan Gasparyan remains inseparable from the international renown of the duduk. Hailed as the master of the duduk, he shared the stage with global artists such as Peter Gabriel, Brian May, Lionel Richie, Michael Brook, and composer Hans Zimmer. Djivan Gasparyan also performed at the 2010 Eurovision Song Contest, accompanying the Armenian entry *Apricot Stone*.

In France and across the diaspora, the practice has flourished thanks to performers such as Levon Minassian, Gérard Madilian, Vardan Grigoryan, Artyom Minasyan, and Haïg Sarikouyoumdjian. The instrument has also crossed into other genres: Didier Malherbe introduced it to jazz with Hadouk Trio, while Gérard Madilian brought it into indie and alternative rock creations with the band Deleyaman starting in 2000. Pedagogical transmission is also taking shape in Europe thanks to Chris Bégot, the first European to graduate from a conservatory in traditional music of Armenia and the Caucasus on the Armenian duduk, who established group classes in Valence in 2022 and Lyon in 2023.

## A Signature Sound Embraced by Global Cinema

Far beyond its homeland, the duduk's deeply distinctive timbre has captivated the global film and television industry. Peter Gabriel was one of the pioneers in introducing it to the screen in Martin Scorsese's *The Last Temptation of Christ* (1988), featuring soloist Vatche Hovsepian. The instrument later achieved massive recognition in Ridley Scott's *Gladiator* (2000), driven by Hans Zimmer's Golden Globe-winning score and Djivan Gasparyan's playing.

The duduk has also resonated in Henri Verneuil's *Mayrig*, Atom Egoyan's *Ararat* with music by Mychael Danna, and in Armand Amar's soundtracks for *Amen.*, *Days of Glory*, and *Live and Become*. More recently, multi-instrumentalist Pedro Eustache showcased its unique colors in John Williams' *Munich* as well as in the two installments of *Dune* scored by Hans Zimmer. Television series such as *Battlestar Galactica* and *Game of Thrones*, along with major video games including *World of Warcraft*, *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim*, and *Uncharted*, now draw upon the deep register and expressive breath of this Armenian oboe to inhabit their imaginary worlds.
