An emblematic hallmark of Armenian art, the khachkar (in Armenian *xač‘k‘ar*, composed of *xač‘*, cross, and *k‘ar*, stone) is not translated as "stone cross," but very precisely as "cross-stone." This linguistic nuance reflects its material reality: it is a rectangular or arched stele, generally carved from local rock such as tuff, basalt, andesite, or sandstone, featuring one or more crosses carved in bas-relief on its west-facing side.

Erected in the open landscape, set on a base using a tenon, embedded in the walls of sanctuaries, or carved directly into the rock, these works most often stand between 1.5 and 2 meters tall. They have weathered the centuries as witnesses to the faith, history, and identity of the Armenian people.

## A Tree of Life: Theology Carved in Stone

The khachkar embodies the Christology unique to the Armenian Apostolic Church, as established in the 8th century under Catholicos Hovhannes III of Odzun. Unlike the tradition of other Christian denominations, the stele does not depict the torment or bodily death of Christ, but affirms his divine nature and his victory over death. The medieval jurist Mkhitar Gosh had already emphasized this distinction, noting that while others honor images, Armenians venerate the cross.

The Armenian cross thus becomes a true tree of life, a celestial sign known as a *nšan*. Its arms flare out into vegetative forms, while its base gives rise to symmetrical leaves that curl into volutes. In its composition, the stele establishes a vertical bridge: the lower register, associated with the earthly world, the past, and human life, connects to the upper celestial and divine realm. Clusters of grapes, pomegranates, and intricate networks of continuously carved interlace create an illusion of infinity. In popular belief, the khachkar is even reputed to house a fragment of the True Cross.

While most steles serve a votive function—seeking salvation for the soul of the donor and their loved ones—others commemorate a foundation, a religious construction, or a military victory. More rarely, some have an apotropaic role (*tsasman*), erected to ward off demons or protect against natural disasters.

## From the Bagratids to the Medieval Golden Age

The history of cross-stones unfolds across four major phases. The roots of the standing stele lie in a long continuum reaching back to *vishap* stones, pre-Arab columns, and monuments. However, true khachkars appeared in the 9th century, spurred by the national revival under the Bagratid dynasty following liberation from the Arab yoke. One of the oldest surviving dated examples was erected at Garni in 879 by Queen Katranide, wife of King Ashot I, echoing other early witnesses such as that of Hortun (876) or the base discovered in the Martakert region (853).

The art of the khachkar reached its zenith between the 12th and 14th centuries. During this period of exceptional technical virtuosity, master craftsmen handled the drill and chisel with lace-like precision. Renowned names emerged, such as Timot and Mkhitar at Geghard (1213), Poghos at Goshavank (1291), Momik in Vayots Dzor, and Vahram at Haghpat (1273). The latter crafted a remarkable *Amenap‘erkič‘* ("Savior of All"), a rare type depicting the Crucifixion or the Descent from the Cross, to which popular devotion attributed healing virtues.

Regional workshops developed distinctive styles: the Artsakh school, for instance at Dadivank and Gtichavank, featured crosses pattée set against a ground of delicate tuff foliage scrolls. Following a lull caused by the Mongol invasions, the art experienced a revival in the 16th and 17th centuries, exemplified notably by works from Syunik and the slender khachkars of the great cemetery of Julfa.

## An Endangered Heritage Recognized by UNESCO

Over the centuries, the khachkar also served as written record. Its meticulous inscriptions in uncial script (*erkatagir*), sometimes carved in relief, specify the identities of sculptors, the year of erection, and donations, providing invaluable historical and linguistic documentation.

Once scattered throughout historic Armenia, these works have suffered immense losses. Between 1998 and 2005, the medieval cemetery of Julfa in Nakhchivan, which numbered several thousand red tuff steles, was completely destroyed and razed in a planned operation by the armed forces of Azerbaijan. Khachkars have also been the target of systematic destruction in Nagorno-Karabakh by Azerbaijan. In Turkey, very few specimens survived the systematic eradication carried out since the Armenian Genocide. Degradations and attempts at cultural erasure have also been documented in Georgia, in the Javakheti region.

To protect and honor this heritage, on November 17, 2010, UNESCO inscribed "Armenian cross-stones art. Symbolism and craftsmanship of Khachkars" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. In the Republic of Armenia, where tens of thousands of examples survive—including the remarkable gathering at the Noratus cemetery on the shores of Lake Sevan—khachkars are classified by law as inalienable cultural property.

## A Living Tradition Radiating to Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes

Far from being an extinct practice, the creation of khachkars has experienced a revival since the 1960s. Contemporary craftsmen perpetuate this stone-carving tradition passed down from generation to generation, and the Armenian diaspora has erected these steles around the world, particularly to honor the departed and commemorate the victims of the genocide. In France, nearly thirty khachkars stand in public spaces.

This long tradition continues to leave its mark on memories and landscapes. Those attached to this millenary culture are invited to a solemn and fraternal moment: the "Blessing of the KHACHKAR in Saint-Chamond" ceremony, which will take place on October 18, 2026, in the heart of the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region.
