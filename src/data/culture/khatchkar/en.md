An iconic hallmark of Armenian art, the khachkar (in Armenian *xač‘k‘ar*, composed of *xač‘*, cross, and *k‘ar*, stone) does not translate as "stone cross," but very precisely as "cross-stone." This linguistic nuance reflects its material reality: it is a rectangular or arched stele, generally carved from local rock such as tuff, basalt, andesite, or sandstone, displaying one or more crosses sculpted in bas-relief on its west-facing side.

Erected outdoors, anchored to a pedestal with a tenon, embedded into the walls of sanctuaries, or carved directly into living rock, these works most often stand between 1.5 and 2 meters high. They have endured through the centuries as witnesses to the faith, history, and identity of the Armenian people.

## A Tree of Life: Theology Carved in Stone

The khachkar embodies the Christology distinctive to the Armenian Apostolic Church, as it was established in the 8th century under Catholicos Hovhannes III of Odzun. Unlike the tradition of other Christian denominations, the stele does not depict the suffering or bodily death of Christ, but rather proclaims his divine nature and his victory over death. The jurist Mkhitar Gosh had already noted this distinction: "the Greeks and Georgians honor images more, the Armenians, the cross."

The Armenian cross thus becomes a true tree of life, a celestial sign called *nšan*. Its arms flare outwards, their ends sometimes turning into trefoils that evoke the botanical nature of wood, while its base blossoms into symmetrical leaves that curl into volutes. In the composition, the stele forms a vertical bridge: the lower section, associated with the earthly world, the past, and human life, connects to the upper celestial and divine realm. Clusters of grapes, pomegranates, and intricate patterns of continuous interlace create an illusion of infinity. In popular belief, the khachkar is even reputed to house a fragment of the True Cross.

While most stelae serve a votive function—imploring salvation for the soul of the patron and their loved ones—others commemorate a foundation, a religious construction, or a military victory. More rarely, some have an apotropaic role (*tsasman*), raised to ward off demons or protect against natural disasters.

## From the Bagratids to the Medieval Golden Age

The history of cross-stones unfolds through four major phases. Some scholars, particularly those trained during the Soviet era, trace the free-standing stele to a long lineage originating from *vishap* stones, early Christian stelae, and pre-Arab-era pillars; its origins, however, remain uncertain. The first true khachkars appeared in the 9th century, spurred by the national revival under the Bagratid dynasty following liberation from the Arab yoke. The one often presented as the oldest dated khachkar was erected in Garni in 879 by Queen Katranide, wife of King Ashot I, although a khachkar dated to 876 is known (Hortun), as well as the base of a lost khachkar bearing the date 853 in the Martakert region.

The art of the khachkar reached its zenith between the 12th and 14th centuries. During this period of extraordinary technical virtuosity, master builders used the drill and chisel with lace-like precision. Renowned masters are associated with masterpieces: Timot and Mkhitar, who are likely credited with the one at Geghard (1213); Poghos at Goshavank (1291); Momik in Vayots Dzor; and Vahram at Haghpat (1273). The latter crafted a remarkable *Amenap‘erkič‘* ("All-Savior"), a rare type depicting the Crucifixion or the Deposition from the Cross, to which popular devotion attributed healing properties.

Regional workshops cultivated their own distinct characteristics: the Artsakh school, for example at Dadivank and Gtichavank, featured pattée crosses set against a background of scrolling rinceaux, carved in fine-grained tuff. After a decline caused by the Seljuk and subsequent Mongol invasions, the art experienced a revival in the 16th and 17th centuries, notably exemplified by works in Syunik or the slender khachkars of the great cemetery of Julfa.

## An Endangered Heritage Recognized by UNESCO

Over the centuries, the khachkar has also served as a written archive. Its meticulous inscriptions in uncial script (*erkatagir*), sometimes carved in relief, record the identities of sculptors, the dates of erection, or donations, providing invaluable historical and linguistic records.

Once scattered throughout historic Armenia, these works have suffered immense losses. Between 1998 and 2005, the medieval cemetery of Julfa in Nakhchivan, which still contained nearly 3,000 red tuff stelae (out of some 10,000 that once stood there), was systematically and entirely razed in an operation carried out by the government of Azerbaijan. Khachkars have also been the target of systematic destruction in Nagorno-Karabakh by Azerbaijan. In Turkey, very few specimens survived the systematic eradication initiated during the Armenian Genocide. Damage and attempts at cultural erasure have also been documented in Georgia, in the Javakheti region.

To safeguard and honor this legacy, UNESCO inscribed "Armenian cross-stones art. Symbolism and craftsmanship of Khachkars" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on November 17, 2010. In the Republic of Armenia, where an estimated 40,000 khachkars survive—including the remarkable gathering at the Noratus cemetery on the shores of Lake Sevan—they are classified by law as inalienable cultural property.

## A Living Tradition Resonating as Far as Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes

Far from being an extinct practice, the creation of khachkars has seen a resurgence since the 1960s. Khachkar carvers can still be seen at work in certain neighborhoods of Yerevan, heirs to a craft passed down from master to apprentice or within families. The Armenian diaspora has planted these stelae across the globe, notably as tributes to the departed and in memory of the victims of the genocide. In France, records indicate that nearly thirty khachkars stand in public spaces.

This long-standing tradition continues to shape memory and the cultural landscape. Those devoted to this thousand-year-old culture are invited to a solemn occasion: the "Blessing of the KHACHKAR in Saint-Chamond" ceremony, which will take place on October 18, 2026, in Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes.
