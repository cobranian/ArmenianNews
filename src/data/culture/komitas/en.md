Born Soghomon Soghomonian on September 26, 1869, in Kütahya in the Ottoman Empire, the man who would become Father Komitas carried within him the history of a people. Orphaned of his mother at just six months old, and then of his father at the age of eleven, within a Turkish-speaking family whose paternal ancestors originated from Goghtn, the young boy experienced early destitution. His singing voice was nevertheless noticed during Sunday services. In 1881, the prelate of his parish took him to the Holy See of Etchmiadzin, where Catholicos George IV had decided to make twenty places available to orphans at the seminary he had founded. Although he did not speak Armenian at the time, Soghomon moved the Catholicos by intoning the hymn *Looys zevart*. Admitted to the Gevorkian Seminary, he learned Armenian, was introduced to choral singing under Kristapor Kara-Murza, and developed a passion for *khaz*, the ancient Armenian neumatic notation.

Ordained a hieromonk in 1894 under the religious name of Komitas—a tribute to a 7th-century Catholicos and musician—and elevated shortly thereafter to the rank of vardapet, he subsequently devoted his life to the service of folk and sacred melodies.

## At the Source of Folk Song and Liturgical Modes

Conscious of the distortion suffered by traditional liturgical monody in the face of polyphonic harmonization imitated from the West, Komitas made it his mission to recover the authentic musical modes of the Armenian liturgy. Supported by Catholicos Mkrtich Khrimian and financed by a scholarship from the philanthropist Alexander Mantashev, he went to complete his training in Tiflis under Makar Yekmalyan, before settling in Berlin between 1896 and 1899. There, he honed his piano playing and studied composition at Richard Schmidt’s private conservatory, then studied musicology at the Frederick William University under specialists such as Oskar Fleischer, Heinrich Bellermann, and Max Friedlaender. He became one of the earliest members of the International Music Society (*Internationale Musikgesellschaft*).

Upon returning to Etchmiadzin, Komitas traveled through the Armenian countryside with seminarians to collect the words and tunes of the peasantry. His approach proved pioneering for ethnomusicology: he observed the genesis of songs in daily life, discovered the practice of polyrhythmic singing, and meticulously noted down even the singers' gestures. More than three thousand melodies were collected in this manner—over half of which have since been lost, with only around 1,200 surviving today—immortalizing secular traditions, songs of love and marriage, lullabies, and dance tunes. In 1903, he published *A Thousand and One Songs*, while also opening up to other cultures of the region with the publication of a pioneering collection, the first anthology of Kurdish songs. This undertaking, however, aroused hostility from a conservative faction of the Etchmiadzin clergy, who mocked him as "the priest who sings of love."

## Constantinople and the Influence of Armenian Art

In 1910, seeking to broaden his audience and escape these clerical tensions, Komitas moved to Constantinople, settling in the Pangaltı neighborhood, where he shared a house with the painter Panos Terlemezian. There he founded the *Gusan* choir, which brought together dozens of musicians, and trained several disciples in musicology, including Barsegh Kanachyan and Mihran Tumacan. His choir performed the folk songs he had harmonized for four voices, popularizing them throughout Armenian communities.

Komitas also disseminated his knowledge through lectures and concerts in Europe. In Paris, his performances were received with great admiration. In 1906, writing in the pages of the *Mercure Musical*, musicologist Louis Laloy praised an art of "graceful sweetness" and "reverent solemnity." In 1912, the composer completed his arrangement of the Divine Liturgy (*Badarak*), whose harmonization has since become standard. Two years later, he attended the Congress of the International Music Society in Paris; his work earned praise from musicians such as Claude Debussy.

## The 1915 Arrest and Exile into Silence

This intense creative journey was brutally interrupted by the Armenian Genocide. On the night of April 24, 1915, Komitas was caught in the roundup of Armenian intellectuals in Constantinople, with which the Ottoman authorities initiated the genocide. Held in the central prison and then forced onto a deportation train, he was taken to the prison in Ayaş, and subsequently placed under house arrest in Çankırı, in Anatolia. During his captivity, he was beaten by guards and shared the agony of his companions, many of whom were later murdered.

Thanks to outside interventions—including those of American ambassador Henry Morgenthau and prominent Turkish intellectuals—Komitas was among a small group of prisoners pardoned by order of Talaat Pasha and permitted to return to Constantinople in May 1915. Upon returning home, he found his library looted and nearly all of his life's work gone. Deeply scarred by the persecutions and the massacres of his compatriots, he developed severe post-traumatic stress disorder. Although he still managed to compose the *Armenian Dances* in the spring of 1916, his mental health deteriorated again that autumn, plunging him into silence.

Admitted to the Hôpital de la Paix in Şişli in the autumn of 1916, he left Constantinople for France in 1919. Treated first at Ville-Évrard, he spent the final years of his life in a psychiatric institution in Villejuif, where he passed away on October 22, 1935.

## An Enduring Presence in Universal Heritage

In 1936, Komitas's ashes were transferred to Yerevan to rest in the Pantheon that now bears his name. The legacy of his work remains immense: by collecting and publishing so many folk songs, he preserved the cultural heritage of Western Armenia, which without him would have vanished with the genocide.

The "Collection of Komitas Vardapet’s Works" is inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register. In Yerevan, the State Conservatory, the main avenue of the Arabkir district, and a museum keep his memory alive, while his portrait has adorned the 10,000-dram banknote since 2018. Chamber music ensembles, vocalists, and pianists continue to record and bring his transcriptions and compositions to life around the world.
