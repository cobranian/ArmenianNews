Tigranes II Artashesian, known to posterity as Tigranes the Great (*Tigran Mets*), born around 140 BC and died in 55 BC, embodies a period of unprecedented brilliance in the history of the Armenian nation. A scion of the Artaxiad dynasty and grandson of the reformer monarch Artaxias I, he reigned from 95 to 55 BC. Under his rule, the Kingdom of Greater Armenia expanded far beyond its traditional borders to establish itself, for several decades, as the preeminent power east of the Roman world.

## From the Shores of Exile to Royal Assertion

Before donning the crown, Prince Tigranes spent long years held hostage at the court of the Parthian king Mithridates II, following a military conflict disadvantageous to the Armenians. Treated with the honors due to his rank, he learned the diplomatic and military workings of eastern courts. In 95 BC, upon the death of his father King Tigranes I, he regained his freedom in exchange for ceding "seventy valleys" of fertile land located in Atropatene and Caspiane.

Crowned at the age of forty-five, Tigranes found a country structured by the legacy of his predecessors. However, the mountainous terrain naturally divided the land into provinces dominated by powerful feudal lords, the *nakharark*. Eager to build a centralized authority, the sovereign consolidated royal power. As early as 94 BC, he deposed Artanes, king of Sophene, permanently annexing that principality to the royal domain. That same year, Tigranes forged a decisive alliance with the King of Pontus, Mithridates VI Eupator, marrying his sixteen-year-old daughter, Cleopatra. The two monarchs outlined their ambitions: Anatolia and the western shores for the Pontic kingdom, expansion toward the East for Armenia. Their combined armies intervened jointly in Cappadocia as early as 93 BC, driving out the client king backed by Rome, Ariobarzanes I.

## The Building of the Empire "From Sea to Sea"

Following the death of Mithridates II of Parthia in 88 BC, Tigranes seized upon the internal divisions within his former suzerain's realm. Repudiating all vassalage, the Armenian army reclaimed the seventy valleys, entered Upper Mesopotamia, and subjugated Atropatene, Adiabene, Gordyene, and Osroene. Pushing as far as Ecbatana, the summer residence of the Parthian kings, Tigranes captured the fortress of Adrabana. King Gotarzes I was forced to submit, and Tigranes assumed the sovereign title of "King of Kings" (*arkayits arka*). To the north, the monarch extended his influence over the kingdoms of Iberia and Caucasian Albania.

In 83 BC, faced with the decline of the Seleucid Empire torn by dynastic rivalries, the notables and Greek cities of Syria chose to offer the crown to Tigranes to restore concord. Appointing Magadates as governor in Antioch, the King of Armenia took possession of Syria, Phoenicia, Cilicia, and Commagene, extending his outposts as far as the city of Ptolemais.

The empire thus reached its territorial zenith, covering an estimated area of 900,000 square kilometers, stretching from the Pontic Alps and the Caucasus to the Mesopotamian borderlands, and from the Caspian Sea to the Mediterranean Sea. This geopolitical reality gave rise to the popular expression of Armenia "from sea to sea" (*tsovits tsov Hayastan*). According to the historian Plutarch, Tigranes appeared in majesty escorted by four vassal kings. The Roman orator Cicero publicly acknowledged that the Armenian monarch had made the Roman Republic tremble through the renown and effectiveness of his armies.

## Tigranakert and Hellenistic Influence

To provide this vast multiethnic realm with a suitable administrative center, Tigranes founded a new metropolis in the 80s and 70s BC: Tigranakert (Tigranocerta), established in the province of Aghdznik along the western Tigris. Other cities bearing his name were also erected, notably in Artsakh. To ensure the prosperity of his new capital, the king relocated thousands of residents there from cities across Cappadocia, Cilicia, and Syria.

Tigranakert adopted a Greek-inspired urban layout, featuring a theater, imposing ramparts, and lavish palaces. Proclaimed a "Philhellene," the king welcomed rhetoricians and philosophers to his court, including Metrodorus of Scepsis, author of a *History of Tigranes*. The administration adopted Greek alongside local traditions, while the Zoroastrian pantheon was gradually assimilated with the Olympian deities.

This dynamism was reflected in the coinage minted in Antioch and Damascus. These silver tetradrachms, admired for the delicacy of their engraving, depicted the king in profile, wearing the Armenian tiara adorned with a star between two birds of prey—symbols of Zoroastrian royal glory—or bearing the radiance of Halley's Comet, observed in 87 BC. The reverse displayed the goddess Tyche of Antioch enthroned above the Orontes River.

## The Clash of Legions and the Peace of Artashat

The inevitable confrontation with Rome arose when Mithridates VI, defeated by Lucullus in 72 BC, took refuge with Tigranes, who refused to surrender his ally and father-in-law. In 69 BC, Roman forces invaded Armenia and laid siege to Tigranakert. On October 6, 69 BC, following the battle and the betrayal of a faction of guards who opened the gates, the capital fell to Lucullus, although Tigranes managed to rescue his family with the aid of a six-thousand-strong cavalry detachment.

The following year, the allied Armenian and Pontic forces held off the legions before the ancient capital, Artashat. Confronted by difficult terrain and mounting casualties, the Roman soldiers mutinied, forcing Lucullus to retreat. Pompey was then tasked with bringing the war to an end in 66 BC. Facing the advance of fresh legions and the rebellion of his own son, Tigranes the Younger, the seventy-five-year-old monarch chose diplomacy.

During his meeting with Pompey, Tigranes renounced his external conquests—Syria, Phoenicia, and Sophene—and paid an indemnity of six thousand silver talents. Pompey raised him with deference and confirmed his authority over the Kingdom of Greater Armenia with the title of ally of Rome. Tigranes thus ruled peacefully over his ancestral lands until his death in 55 BC at the age of eighty-five, leaving the throne to his son Artavasdes II. From the admiring pen of Moses of Chorene to the baroque operas composed in the eighteenth century by Antonio Vivaldi and Christoph Willibald Gluck, the epic of Tigranes remains one of the most brilliant touchstones of Armenian national memory.
